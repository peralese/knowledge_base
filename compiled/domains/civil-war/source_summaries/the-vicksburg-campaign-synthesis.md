# Summary

The Vicksburg Campaign, spanning from November 1862 to July 1863, was a pivotal military operation during the American Civil War. Led by Union Major General Ulysses S. Grant against Confederate Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton, this campaign resulted in Union control over the Mississippi River, effectively bisecting the Confederacy and marking a significant turning point in the war. The strategic importance of Vicksburg's location on the Mississippi River was highlighted by President Lincoln as key to dividing Confederate forces and disrupting their supply lines. The challenging terrain around Vicksburg required innovative operational solutions due to its bluffs, bayous, and floodplains.

Grant employed a combination of strategic maneuvers, including early attempts such as Sherman’s Chickasaw Bayou attack and the Holly Springs raid, which faced setbacks but demonstrated his determination. The campaign saw significant Union victories at Port Gibson, Raymond, Jackson, Champion Hill, and Big Black River, leading to Pemberton's retreat into Vicksburg. Grant adapted his strategy by collaborating with Admiral Porter to use naval forces in flanking maneuvers around Vicksburg.

The subsequent siege warfare involved a six-week battle characterized by trench assaults and steady bombardment from Union artillery and naval forces. After 47 days, Pemberton surrendered on July 4, 1863, resulting in Union control of the Mississippi River, which was crucial for disrupting Confederate logistics and communications while boosting Union morale. The campaign highlighted Grant’s operational flexibility, joint operations with naval assets, and effective command structure compared to Confederate indecision.
