Build Your AI-Powered Morning Brief
Dave Kline
@dklineii
·
This is a skills file. If it reads like the work you already do as a leader, that's because it
is. The reality is that you do much of this intuitively. If you have time to do it at all. But
since it's a skills file, you can copy and paste this article into your LLM. Have it apply
the concepts and ask you questions to tailor it into your personalized morning brief.
Prep the meeting. Track commitments. Coach your execution. The LLM will even help
you refine the skills file for you over time. It's how a chat becomes a Chief of Staff.
Most managers don't start the day. Their day starts them.
The typical move. Roll over, open email on the phone before both feet are on the floor.
Answer three messages that felt urgent and were not. Walk into the first meeting
having read the invite title and nothing else. Discover at 2 pm that the one thing that
actually mattered today never got your attention because the inbox ate your morning.
By 6 pm, you've answered everything and moved nothing.
Leaders don't react. They prepare.
That is the whole skill. The fix is one move. Before you touch the inbox, you review
your brief. Fifteen minutes, same time every morning, where the AI reads your day,
your decisions, your people, and the world, and hands you back a clear plan instead of
a chaotic pile.
That move compounds three ways.
1. You enter every room already knowing why you're there and what you want out
of it, so you stop paying the cold-open tax on six meetings a day.
2. You catch the problems that need your attention before they catch fire and take
10x your time to put them out.


3. And you spend your most productive time on your most important work (while
you're at your best), and not on whichever email made your phone vibrate most
recently.
I've gotten this down to fifteen minutes each day. One recurring calendar block at 8:00.
The brief reads from the tools you already live in. It never makes you log into anything.
The output fits on one screen.
The Six Reads
A morning brief is not a to-do list and it is not the news. It is six reads of your actual
day, in this order.
The Day. Your calendar and your task list, collapsed into one view. What is scheduled,
what is due, and where are the open hours?
The Decisions. What is stuck on you right now? The approvals, the calls, the replies
that someone is waiting on. The brief surfaces these so you make them early, when
your judgment is sharpest, not at 6pm when it isn't.
The People. Who on your team needs you today? Who has a 1:1 and what they
committed to last time? Who has gone quiet? Who is out? This is the read most
managers skip, and it is the one only a leader can act on.
The Meetings. Every meeting on the calendar, each with one line. Why it exists, the
outcome you want, the one question to ask. Slow starts become fast decisions.
The World. Three to five things that happened outside your walls that your team will
care about. Not a news feed. The specific client, competitor , or shift that touches the
work in front of you.
The Move. The single change to today that gives you the most leverage toward your
north star. Not a list. One move. The meeting to decline so a two-hour block opens for

the work that actually compounds. The conversation to have today instead of next
week. The brief names the one move and writes the message that makes it happen.
Six reads. The Day, the Decisions, the People, the Meetings, the World, the Move. Run in
order, they take the AI about a minute to assemble and take you fifteen to act on.
Prepare before you react.
It is always tempting to jump straight into email. The messages are right there; they
feel urgent, and clearing them feels like progress. But the moment you open the inbox
first, you have let other people decide what matters today. You spend your freshest
hour on their priorities instead of your own.
The discipline is simple. Commit the first 15 minutes of the day to reading and
applying your morning brief, before anything reactive. Do that, and you enter the day
prepared to advance your priorities, not reacting to everyone else's. Skip it, and the day
starts you.
Four rules govern a brief you can trust. They are not optional. A brief that breaks them
is worse than no brief, because it reads with false confidence.
1. Never make a theoretical suggestion. "Protect your mornings" is useless. "Move the
2:30 vendor call to Thursday so your 9-to-11 block stays clear for the board deck" is a
brief. Every recommendation names the actual meeting, the actual time, the actual move.
2. Never stop because something is missing. If a connector is down or a meeting has no
agenda, the brief flags the hole and keeps going. It never returns a blank because one
input failed.
3. Never take a shortcut. If the brief says it researched the person you're meeting, it
actually pulled their company and their recent news. Summarizing the meeting title is
not research.
4. Never make anything up. If the brief doesn't know who called a meeting or what a
metric did last week, it says so. A flagged unknown is useful. A confident guess is a
landmine.

Hand these four rules to the AI once, at the top of your brief prompt, and they govern
every read underneath.
Build the skill. Let it run while you sleep.
Here is where the leverage comes from. You do not run these prompts by hand every
morning. You paste this entire article into a tool like Claude Cowork and tell it to build
you a morning-brief skill from it. Then you connect your calendar, email, chat, tasks,
and metrics, and you schedule that skill to run overnight. By the time you wake up, the
brief is already written and waiting on one screen.
The six prompts below are the engine of the skill. The first builds the brief. The next
three sharpen the reads that take real work: your people, your meetings, the world.
The last two grade today and clear yesterday's loose ends. Read them once so you
know what your overnight brief is doing on your behalf. Then hand the article to the AI,
point it at your tools, and let it assemble and schedule the whole thing.
Prompt 1. Build the brief
This is the load-bearing prompt. It reads your connectors and returns the Six Reads on
one screen.
You are my Chief of Staff. Build my morning brief for today. Read my calendar, my email,
my task list, my team chat, and my metrics dashboard. Return six sections in this order.
The Day: my schedule and what's due, with open blocks marked. The Decisions:
everything currently blocked on me, ordered by cost of delay. The People: who on my
team has a 1:1 today and what they committed to last time, who has gone quiet, who is
out. The Meetings: every meeting with one line each (why it exists, the outcome I want,
the one question to ask). The World: leave a placeholder, I'll fill it with a separate prompt.
The Move: the single highest-leverage change I can make to today to advance my north
star, which is [paste your north star or top priority]. Name one specific move (the meeting
to decline, the block to protect, the conversation to pull forward) and draft the message
that makes it happen. Follow four rules. Name actual times and actual meetings, never
give generic advice. If a source is unavailable, flag it and keep going. Don't summarize

when you can research. If you don't know something, say so instead of guessing. Keep the
whole brief to one screen.
The first time you run it, the brief will flag what it can't see. That list is the setup
checklist for tomorrow. Connect those sources once and the brief gets sharper every
morning.
Prompt 2. The people radar
The Day read optimizes you. This one points the same tool at your team. It is the
prompt that separates a manager's brief from an executive's productivity app.
Look only at the People read. Based on my last two weeks of 1:1 notes, team chat, and
who I've actually spent time with, tell me the one person who most needs my attention
today and why. Consider who committed to something last time and owes an update, who
has gone quiet relative to their normal pattern, and who is carrying a load I haven't
checked on. Give me the person, the reason, and the specific 90-second move I should
make today (the message to send, the question to ask in our 1:1, the hallway check-in). If
you don't have enough signal to call it, tell me what you'd need to watch.
This is the prompt most managers will skip because the inbox feels more urgent. Run it
anyway. The person who goes quiet in June is the resignation in August. The brief is the
cheapest early-warning system you will ever own.
Prompt 3. Prep every meeting and research the outsiders
Cold opens are a tax you pay in credibility. This prompt pre-pays it.
Go meeting by meeting through today's calendar. For each internal meeting, confirm the
one-line purpose, desired outcome, and the question I should ask. For each meeting with
someone outside my company, actually research them: pull their role, their company,
what their company has done or announced recently, and any prior thread between us.
Don't summarize the calendar invite and call it research. Give me three bullets per
external attendee and the one thing I should reference to show I did my homework. Flag
any meeting that has no clear purpose, so I can decide whether to send an agenda or
cancel it.

The external research is the line managers cut when they're busy, and it's the line that
costs deals and first impressions. Make the AI do the legwork. You walk in informed
without spending the night before on it.
Prompt 4. Scan the world for your team
The World read, done right. This is intelligence, not a news habit.
Find three to five things from the last 48 hours that my team specifically should know
about. Anchor the search to my team's domain, our top accounts, and the competitors
and shifts that touch our current work. For each item, give me the headline, a one-
sentence why-it-matters-to-us, and the link. Actually search and read the sources. Do not
invent headlines or fabricate links. If you can only find two that genuinely matter, give me
two. I'd rather have two real ones than five filler ones.
Two real signals beat five generic ones. The point isn't to be informed about
everything. It's to walk into your team's standup already holding the one thing they're
about to ask you about.
The mistakes that kill the brief
1. Opening the inbox first. The single failure that breaks everything downstream.
Once email has set your agenda, the brief is a post-mortem. The block goes
before the inbox or the skill doesn't work.
2. Letting it run on theory. A brief that says "focus on what matters" is noise. If it
isn't naming a specific meeting and a specific time, send it back. Generic output
trains you to ignore it.
3. Skipping the People read. A brief that only optimizes your calendar is a
productivity app. The leverage a manager has and a solo operator doesn't is
people. Cut this read and you've automated the wrong half of your job.
4. Trusting a brief that hides its gaps. If the AI guesses instead of flagging what it
couldn't see, you'll act on fiction. Demand the flags. A brief that says "I couldn't
reach your metrics dashboard" is doing its job.

5. Reading it and changing nothing. The brief that surfaces the one move and
doesn't make it is theater. The Move read exists to be acted on before 9am, while
the calendar is still soft.
6. Making it long. A brief that doesn't fit on one screen won't get read on a
Tuesday. If it's growing, the reads are getting verbose, not the day getting more
complex. Cut it back.
7. Running it only on hard days. The brief compounds because it's daily. Skip it
on the calm mornings and you lose the pattern that lets it spot the quiet team
member and the drifting priority.
Close the loops and compound.
The morning brief points forward. But the best version also reaches back one day and
clears the small debts before they pile up. Two more prompts, run at the end of the
brief.
Prompt 5. Grade the day and fix it
The truth-teller prompt, and the engine behind the Move read. It grades the day you
have against your north star, and it only speaks in moves.
Grade today's calendar against my north star, which is [paste your north star or top
priority]. Tell me how many minutes today actually advance it. If the answer is near zero,
find me the time. Name the specific meeting to move, decline, shorten, or delegate, and
tell me who should attend in my place if I step out. Give me the exact reschedule (this
meeting, to this open block). Then give me one sentence I can send to make each change.
Be direct. This is the prompt I'd skip because it tells me my day is built wrong.
This is the prompt that turns a brief from a mirror into a coach. A mirror shows you the
day. A coach tells you the day is built wrong and hands you the three messages that fix
it. Send them before 9am, while the calendar still bends.
Prompt 6. Close yesterday's loops and draft ahead

The legwork prompt. It clears the small commitments and pre-writes the obvious
replies so the day starts with momentum, not a backlog.
Two jobs. First, look at what I committed to yesterday across email, chat, and my 1:1
notes, and list what I owe today and to whom. Flag anything already slipping. Second,
draft the two or three messages today will obviously require: the recap from yesterday's
meeting, the reply to the question I keep getting, the follow-up I promised. Write them in
my voice as drafts I can edit and send in two minutes. Don't send anything. Just stage it. If
you're not sure I actually committed to something, flag it as a maybe rather than
asserting it.
This is the prompt that buys back the first hour. The recap that would have taken
twenty minutes at lunch is staged before you've had coffee. You edit and send. The loop
closes while it's still cheap to close.
A note on the skills file
You already do a version of this. You glance at your calendar in the elevator. You think
about who you're worried about on the drive in. You mean to read up on the client
before the call and run out of time.
The brief doesn't add a new habit. It takes the one you already have and makes it
complete, and it runs in fifteen minutes instead of leaking across your whole morning.
And it reviews all the information you don't have time to.
Handing this file to your LLM is how you start transforming your chatbot into your
Chief of Staff.
